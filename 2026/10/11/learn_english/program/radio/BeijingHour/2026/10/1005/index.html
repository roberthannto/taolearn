One was when I was approaching age 40, I said to myself, you know, I gotta get out of manufacturing because Chinese people are very good at it now, and I’ve gotta find something else to do. So I said to myself, I’ll do the finance sector and I’ll do venture capital. So together with two friends of mine, we set up an investment fund. You know, I put a lot of my money into it.

So in English, we say I had skin in the game. And then also we weren’t being paid very much. So we had also, you know, sweat equity. And what we were doing is we were investing in young Chinese entrepreneurs and their teams to develop businesses based on the mobile sector. So we were taking a real risk because all of these businesses, you know, were basically startup businesses where they had no revenue at the beginning. And then we were trying to build them into a business that could then grow over time. So that was a big risk. The other one was, at the end of my career in China. So I went down to Citibank in Hong Kong and I put myself into an, what’s called an air finance school. So they were teaching bankers how to basically finance commercial aircraft. While I was there, I ran into a guy who was investing in aging A320, Airbus A320 aircraft in Europe, and then they were disassembling them and selling the parts. So it was basically the junkyard business. To me, that was a great idea. And so the last thing I did in China was I ended up running a salvage business in Beijing, where we were buying. Air China, old 747s and disassembling them to sell the parts.

Right. You moved out of China in, altogether in 2015.

My wife and I had spent over 20 years there. We basically felt that our children were ready to go to college and. And so they were going to go to college in the United States. And then the second thing was that we were getting to an age where we felt it was important that if we returned to America, we needed time to be in a community where we could make new friends. So we decided in, at that time to leave, and we ended up here in the Bay Area near San Francisco.

Okay. Of course, you spend a lot of your career in China. Did it take some getting used to moving back to the US?

Yes. You know, a lot of people ask us, like, what was the hardest thing about coming back to America. Surprisingly, it was this, is that when we lived in China, we were speaking Chinese and we were engaged in a foreign culture. I love the food, by the way, you know. So every day was very engaging. When we came to America, we were faced with the much more routine life of everybody else in America. Take your kids to school, help them with their sports, do the chores and everything. And so we found that the routine of America was mentally harder to adjust to the excitement of living, you know, abroad in China.

I’m also particularly interested in your travels in China. Of course, you’ve been all over the place. You said you’re mainly based in Chongqing area. And back in those days when you were in China, tell us a little bit more how all these travels kind of like boosted your, maybe your understanding about China.

Yeah, so in the early days, I don’t know if you can remember, but so in the early days, you know, trains in China were very slow. They would travel at maybe 30 kilometers per hour rather than 300 km per hour. So I was in the machine tool industry at the time. We would go out to factories in the far west. So part of the reason there were factories in the west was one was Yuman was an oil drilling facility at the western end of the Great Wall. So I went out there with a machine installer and we rode on a steam engine for about 50 hours. And so it was easier to learn Chinese at that time because, you know, I had basically two days on the train to talk to the fellow passengers in the machine tool world. I did a lot of traveling. I went to Chichi Har. I went to Harbin. I went to a lot of places in what we, in English, we say the Rust Belt. You know, so the heavy industry parts of China, I spent a lot of time in Wuxi in my early days. As I said, I spent three years in Chengdu and then much later I went and started traveling down to Nantong and down into Guangxi and Guangdong. So my experience of southern China is pretty recent. Like, you know, Shenzhen, I never saw Shenzhen when it was a small village. I saw Shenzhen when it was a city of 20 million people.

Right. If you don’t mind me asking, what sort of food do you like most?

So I like all Chinese food. My family lived in Chengdu in Sichuan for three years. And so we definitely like the vegetables and the hot and spicy aspect of Sichuan food.

Right. Interesting.

Of course, you’ve been working with both Chinese and American partners for decades. I’m just curious, what differences have you actually noticed in the way US and Chinese entrepreneurs approach innovation, risk and business building?

So, you know, I think one thing to just mention is that I think for US entrepreneurs here in the US market versus Chinese entrepreneurs in the Chinese market. The Chinese market is much more competitive, is much more, you know, changes, everything changes much more quickly. So my feeling is that in observing American entrepreneurs, they’re very good at innovation, but they’re slower at commercialization. Whereas the Chinese entrepreneurs tend to be more adaptive to what’s going on in the market and also more adapted to their competitors are doing. And so Chinese entrepreneurs will be more flexible in adopting new products or business models to make money, so that’s an important distinction.

The other thing I would say is that US entrepreneurs will tend to avoid crowded markets, you know, markets where there’s a lot of fragmentation and a lot of players and will avoid low return. And in China, partly by the nature of the Chinese market, the Chinese market already is very crowded and the returns are relatively low. So a Chinese entrepreneur or basically focuses on scaling up their business as fast as they can. They hit upon some internet idea, they need to scale it very quickly in order to protect their business.

Right now, renewable is actually constitute a major source of China’s energy supply as we all know. What opportunities do you see for business operating in China in this field today?

Just a perspective back from, let’s say, almost 20 years ago. So when I was working at Beijing, in that period, our daily AQI was over 150. Now when I travel to China, I don’t have any impression that the air quality is anywhere as bad as it was back then. The other thing I would say, the young people of China have strongly support sustainability and environmental protection. And you can see it in terms of the things they do in their lives, in terms of the packaging they want to use or the, you know, the different practices they use. So I think the younger generation in China is going to be very supportive of a green transition. China has done wonders in terms of reducing, you know, fossil fuels, switching to renewables. China’s also done wonders in terms of zero emission cars. I mean, China is the biggest manufacturer of zero emission cars. And China’s probably done a lot of things in terms of sustainable agriculture and reforestation. The interesting thing for China will be now to how do you apply AI to optimize things like transportation, to improve recycling, to predict severe weather. So AI will be an interesting tool for the Chinese to use to maybe even speed up their green transition.

Right. This is a huge problem. I think not only this green transition but also climate change. Do you think there’s enough room for both Beijing and Washington to cooperate on this field and other a wider area.

I do. I do. So I’m a believer the long term direction is towards a green transition worldwide. So I see a situation where renewables from China will be installed here in the US, hopefully. We already have solar panel factories that have been invested in the US by Chinese manufacturers. We have wind turbine capacity that the Chinese could also supply from the US. I think that on the application of AI to the green transition, I don’t see any reason why we would not cooperate. It’s in everybody’s interest to try to have a more sustainable approach to our environment.

Right today, China and the United States, they are increasingly described as this strategic or geopolitical competitors. So how do you understand this question from your perspective?

So look, I mean, I think I accept that China is a world leader and will continue to be a world leader in electric vehicles and batteries and clean energy, in AI, in robotics, in drones, in fast fashion. The list goes on and on. I think that we in America should accept China will always be at that leading edge. And that really what’s important, I think, for both countries is from an economic perspective, it’s very important that we support Chinese companies to invest in the US industries and US to continue to invest in China, and I think those ties are stronger than. If China is trying to export to the US, so we’re trying to export to China. If it’s just purely trade, it’s not as substantive as if we had Chinese investment here in the US I’ll make a comment that long ago, think back to 40 years ago, the Japanese were coming into the US market with cars from Toyota, Nissan and Honda and all kinds of consumer electronics. We were able to arrange for them to actually build car factories in the US and so today, when you look at Japanese cars in America, they’re basically on an equal status with America, you know, GM, Ford and Chrysler, it’s, they’re all part of the same industry. So my hope is that over time, Chinese companies will be citizens here in the US and will be on an equal status.

Right. But this definitely is a misunderstanding between our two countries.

Yes.

What would you say is the biggest aspect? A lot of people in the US get wrong about China? What do they mostly misunderstand China about?

Yeah, okay.

So I’ll say two things. One thing, just like an additional thing came to mind. So unfortunately, you know, I have to apologize because I think the media in America often paints China in a more negative light than what is reality. One thing Americans might not realize is that Chinese are good, friendly people. And every time I travel to China, I’m always impressed with the people I meet. They’re not political. We have conversations about their families and their daily lives. So, you know, that’s just a revelation that if you’re sitting in America and you’re watching TV, you might get an overly negative view about the people of China. The other thing I would say, that a lot of people on the business side don’t realize is that China is not a planned economy controlled by the central government. In fact, the central government is often a follower of trends in China that are driven private companies. So my point here is that many of the more important sectors of China business and employment is driven by private companies and private entrepreneurs. And in fact, if you look at the tech sector, everything that kicked off in electric vehicles and batteries and all that stuff. For the most part, has been driven by private entrepreneurs. So I think that’s a good thing. The country has benefited from a GDP where the majority of value added has actually been from a private sector. And a lot of Americans don’t realize that.

Yeah, I just wanna add another one question actually. Now you’re based in California and you’re kind of like a bringing people together to actually providing services to Chinese enterprises, who want to invest in the US and also US businesses who want to invest in China. Do you still see this continued interest among Chinese and American business communities in each other’s markets?

Yeah, I have a lot of frustration because, you know, I mentioned that the media in America often paints a negative picture of China and that can be discouraging to the younger generation of Americans. My desire is that the younger generation in America should find ways to engage with China. To travel to China, to maybe study in China, to find jobs with Chinese companies. They can find jobs with Chinese companies that are investing outside of China or even in China. So it’s discouraging because I think the younger generation in America is sort of turned off to studying Chinese.

From a business perspective, I think all the businesses that we care about still engage with China. I mean, there’s no question. Apple is China, Nvidia is China. So there’s a big reliance on the Chinese market, partly because China is a leader in tech. One of the things I do is. I sell software to Chinese airlines to connect them to their supplier networks. This is a software company that’s actually owned by airlines around the world, so it’s pretty much an IT solution created by airlines, for airlines on the car side, in terms of some of the big sectors in the economy, to me, the answer is very simple. Let’s try to get BYD to make cars in America. And right now, the political situation is not a good one. So, you know, in America, we choose sides, whether you’re Republican or Democrat. I can’t comment very much on which side I support.
